# Question Bank

The seven categories are the standing frame. They don't change. What changes is how each one gets instantiated for a specific trade, market, and role.

Generic versions of these questions are listed below only to show the shape. **Never send the generic version.** Rewrite each one with something concrete in it — a season, a housing type, a road, a brand, a price band, a specific failure.

---

## The seven categories

### 1. Pre-purchase questions
What customers ask before they buy or book.

Generic shape: *What do people always ask before they'll commit?*

Better: *When somebody calls about a [specific service], what's the first thing they want to know before they'll book?*

Why it matters: these are literal search queries in the customer's own words. This category produces the highest volume of usable content.

### 2. Misconceptions
What customers believe that is wrong.

Generic shape: *What do customers get wrong?*

Better: *What's something homeowners are convinced is true about [thing] that you have to correct on almost every visit?*

Why it matters: a correction is inherently information gain. It's content nobody can write without the expert.

### 3. Unusual cases
Weird jobs, memorable projects, things that surprised even them.

Generic shape: *What's the strangest job you've handled?*

Better: *What's the weirdest thing you've found on a job this year — something the customer had no idea was going on?*

Why it matters: specificity comes free with a story. This is the category that produces detail nothing else does.

### 4. Mistakes
What competitors do wrong, and what customers do to themselves.

Generic shape: *What mistakes do you see?*

Better: *What's the most common thing you see where somebody else worked on it first and made it worse?* / *What do people try themselves that you wish they wouldn't?*

Why it matters: doubles as trust-building and as differentiation from competitors who won't say it.

### 5. Decision criteria
What changes the recommendation.

Generic shape: *How do you decide what to recommend?*

Better: *Two customers call with what sounds like the same problem. What makes you recommend a repair for one and a replacement for the other?*

Why it matters: this is the material that answers real buyer questions and it's almost never on competitor sites.

### 6. Local conditions
Geography, housing stock, climate, code, construction era.

Generic shape: *Does location affect the work?*

Better: *Is there a part of the service area where you see a particular problem more than anywhere else, and do you know why?*

Why it matters: this is what makes location pages genuinely local instead of a template with the city name swapped. Highest-value category for local SEO, and the one most often skipped.

### 7. What they wish customers asked
The questions that would have saved everyone time.

Generic shape: *What do you wish customers asked?*

Better: *What question do you wish people asked before they call, that would have saved them money or trouble?*

Why it matters: usually surfaces the single best piece of content in the whole interview.

## Role weighting

Same seven categories, different emphasis per person. Interviewing everyone about everything produces overlap and wastes rounds.

| Role | Lead with | Skip or de-emphasize |
|---|---|---|
| **Owner** | Decision criteria, cost factors, what they wish customers asked, business philosophy | Day-to-day technical detail |
| **Estimator / Salesperson** | Decision criteria, objections, pre-purchase questions, cost factors | Post-sale process detail |
| **Technician / Installer** | Unusual cases, misconceptions, mistakes customers make, local conditions | Pricing strategy |
| **CSR / Office manager** | Pre-purchase questions, recurring complaints, customer vocabulary | Technical explanation |
| **Project manager** | Where jobs go sideways, decision criteria, local conditions | Pre-sale objections |

The non-obvious people are frequently the richest. Everyone reaches for the owner. The receptionist knows what people ask before booking; the technician knows what customers misunderstand; the CSR knows which complaints keep coming back. Those roles are sitting on first-party data nobody has asked them for.

---

## Round design

**Round 1** — spread across three or four categories to find where this person is richest. Some experts are storytellers, some are precise and terse. You're calibrating.

**Round 2** — go deep on whatever produced the best material in Round 1, plus follow-ups on anything thin.

**Round 3** — fill remaining categories, chase specifics on anything still vague.

By Round 4 you're usually getting repetition. Move to a different person at the same client instead.

---

## Phrasing rules

- Three to five questions per round, no exceptions
- One question per question — no compounds
- Plain language, their vocabulary, no marketing register
- Ask for a story, not a summary
- Never ask something already answered in the expertise file
- If a question could be answered by any company in the trade, it's too generic — rewrite it
